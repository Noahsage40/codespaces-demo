## 1. Hook (0:00 – 0:55)

[Fast montage: "net worth" headlines with "$100 MILLION" circled in red. Short, low-volume clip of a toy-unboxing thumbnail.]

Search "Ryan's World net worth" and you'll get the same number again and again: one hundred million dollars. A kid who started reviewing toys at three years old, now worth a hundred million.

[Counter ticking up: "60,000,000,000+ views".]

His channel has more than sixty billion views [S7]. Millions of people have seen that hundred-million figure and believed it.

But I went through the Forbes lists, the toy sales, the ad rates and the laws on who gets a child's money, and I ran the numbers myself. And the hundred million doesn't mean what you think it means.

[Title card: DID RYAN'S WORLD REALLY MAKE $100 MILLION?]

By the end of this video you'll know where that number came from, what it leaves out, and how much of it could actually belong to Ryan. And I'll give you my own estimate at the end.

## 2. Why it matters: the board (0:55 – 1:50)

[Build "THE BOARD" graphic one row at a time.]

First, here's what the claim would mean. Here's the board.

[Board, column "The claim":]
- Ryan's personal fortune: $100 million
- His age when he started: 3
- How he made it: toy videos

If that's true, a child would be one of the richest creators alive, richer than most movie stars, and every dollar of it would be his.

[Highlight the "every dollar is his" line.]

That last part is what matters, because there are really three different numbers hiding in this story: how much the *brand* sold, how much the *family business* earned, and how much belongs to *Ryan*. Most headlines mix all three.

Keep this board in your head. We're coming back to it.

## 3. Building the case (1:50 – 3:40)

[Timeline graphic: 2015 → today.]

Quick backstory. The channel started in 2015 as Ryan ToysReview [S8]. It's run by his parents through a family production company called Sunlight Entertainment, based in Houston, Texas [S8][S9].

[Pop-up: "Ryan ToysReview → Ryan's World (2019)".]

It grew fast. So fast that Forbes named him the highest-paid YouTuber in the world three years in a row.

[Forbes figures stack onto the screen one by one.]

Twenty-two million dollars on the 2018 list. Twenty-six million in 2019. Twenty-nine and a half million in 2020 [S1][S2][S3]. Then twenty-seven million in 2021, when he dropped to seventh [S4].

Now here's the first clue. Add those up.

[Sum animates: 22 + 26 + 29.5 + 27 = $104.5M.]

One hundred and four and a half million dollars. So that's probably where the hundred million comes from. Case closed?

Not quite. Because I read what Forbes actually measures.

[Zoom on the Forbes methodology line.]

Those figures are *pre-tax*. They are *before* fees to agents, managers and lawyers [S2]. They are estimates built from tracking data and industry interviews, not audited accounts [S1]. And they cover the whole business: ads, sponsorships, licensing and merchandise.

Second clue: the "net worth" sites. Most articles that say "a hundred million" trace back to one estimate on a celebrity net-worth site [S10]. Other trackers put the figure at around twenty to thirty million [S10]. When the same number appears everywhere because everyone copied it, that's not confirmation.

Third clue: in 2019, a consumer watchdog called Truth in Advertising filed a complaint with the US Federal Trade Commission about sponsored videos on the channel [S11]. The FTC confirmed it received the complaint but didn't say whether it opened an investigation [S11]. That same year, YouTube changed how kids' channels get paid. And that brings us to my test.

## 4. My own test (3:40 – 6:30)

[Graphic: "MY TEST" with three buckets: ADS / LICENSING / WHAT'S LEFT.]

I'm going to take one Forbes year, June 2019 to June 2020, the $29.5 million year, and try to rebuild it from scratch.

### Bucket 1: YouTube ads

[Calculator graphic.]

Forbes says the channel got about 12.2 billion views in that period [S3].

In 2019, Google and YouTube paid a $170 million settlement over children's privacy, and YouTube stopped showing personalised ads on videos made for kids [S5]. Kids' content can only show general ads now, and those pay much less. One rights-management firm reported a kids' channel's revenue falling by more than half even as its views went up [S6].

So what's a thousand kids' views worth to the creator? Nobody publishes an exact figure, so I'll use a range: seventy-five cents to two dollars per thousand views. That's my assumption, not a sourced number.

[On screen: 12.2B ÷ 1,000 × $0.75 = $9.2M … × $2 = $24.4M.]

That gives anywhere from about nine million to twenty-four million dollars from ads.

### Bucket 2: Toys and licensing

[Shelf photo of Ryan's World products, then a funnel graphic.]

In 2018, Ryan's World toys launched at Walmart, then reached Target and Amazon [S12]. By the end of 2020, there were around ten thousand licensed products, and the brand was projected to reach half a billion dollars in retail sales [S13].

But here's the catch: half a billion in toy sales is *not* half a billion for the Kajis.

[Funnel: $500M retail → ~$250M wholesale → ~$25M royalties → split.]

Stores keep their markup. As a rule of thumb, the wholesale price is roughly half of the shelf price, so call it $250 million. Licensing royalties are paid on wholesale, and for character brands they average about ten percent [S14]. That's about $25 million in royalties, and that has to be shared with pocket.watch, the studio that manages the brand's licensing [S12][S15].

The split between pocket.watch and the family has never been made public. But a Forbes writer said Ryan's share of the licensing alone made up more than half of his $29.5 million [S3].

### Does it match?

[Two bars side by side: "My rebuild" vs "Forbes".]

Take "more than half" of $29.5 million from licensing. That leaves at most around $14 million for ads and everything else. That only works if the ad rate was near the *bottom* of my range, around a dollar per thousand views.

So my rebuild lines up with Forbes, but only if the post-2019 kids' ad cuts hit hard. Which is exactly what the reporting says happened.

### Bucket 3: What's left

[Waterfall chart: $104.5M → fees → taxes → ≈ $50M.]

Now let's go from gross to what's actually kept. Start with the $104.5 million from the four Forbes lists.

Take off fees for agents, managers and lawyers. Forbes doesn't. Ten to twenty percent is a common range, so I'll use fifteen. That's about $89 million.

Then income tax. The top US federal rate is thirty-seven percent. Texas has no state income tax, which helps. After that: roughly $56 million.

And that's before paying the staff, the studio, editing and production. One report describes a team of around thirty people [S9]. Even a modest allowance for that brings the figure to about fifty million dollars, or less.

## 5. The reveal (6:30 – 7:40)

[Dramatic beat. Black screen. Number fades in.]

So here's my number. Over those four Forbes years, the Ryan's World *business* likely kept somewhere around **forty-five to fifty-five million dollars** after fees and tax.

And that's the business, not Ryan.

[Three stacked boxes: BRAND SALES / FAMILY BUSINESS / RYAN.]

Who actually owns it? The company is run by his parents [S8][S9]. In California, a law called the Coogan Law requires fifteen percent of a child performer's earnings to go into a protected trust, and since 2025 that covers child influencers too [S16]. Illinois, Utah, Minnesota and several other states have passed similar rules [S16][S17].

But the family is based in Texas. In the state-by-state roundups I found, Texas isn't listed as having a law like that [S16][S17]. So there may be no legal minimum that has to be set aside for Ryan. Some reports say his parents set up trusts for him and his siblings [S9], but I couldn't find an amount from a reliable source, so I won't guess.

[On screen: "Ryan's personal share: NOT PUBLIC".]

The honest answer is that nobody outside the family knows what Ryan personally has.

Caveats: every number here is an estimate. The Forbes periods don't line up perfectly and leave a gap in 2020, the years before 2018 and after 2021 aren't counted, and the family runs other channels too. Real lifetime earnings could be higher. But the gross business total and the personal fortune are still two very different numbers.

## 6. Back to the board (7:40 – 8:30)

[Return to THE BOARD. Add a second column, "What I found".]

Remember the board?

- "Ryan's personal fortune: $100 million" → The **business** grossed over $100 million. After fees and tax, my estimate is about $50 million kept. Ryan's personal share isn't public.
- "Started at 3" → True [S8].
- "Made it from toy videos" → Mostly from **licensing and merchandise**, not the videos themselves [S3].

[Red line through "$100M fortune". Green tick next to "$100M+ gross".]

So did Ryan's World make a hundred million dollars? The brand, almost certainly yes, in gross revenue. Did Ryan *get* a hundred million dollars? There's no evidence of that, and the maths says it's very unlikely.

## 7. Fair ending (8:30 – 9:20)

[Slow, calm visuals: generic graphs, YouTube logo, courthouse/legislature b-roll.]

To be clear, this isn't an attack on Ryan or his family. Ryan is a kid. Building a brand that big is a real achievement, and nothing here suggests anyone broke the law.

My issue is with the number. "A hundred million dollars" turns a family business's gross revenue into a child's bank balance, and those aren't the same thing.

And there's a bigger question: when a child is the face of the business, who protects their share? More states are starting to write laws for exactly this [S16]. That's probably the most important thing in this whole story.

[Final card: MY PICK.]

My final pick: Ryan's World grossed more than $100 million. The business kept about half of that. And Ryan's own share is something only his family knows.
